Some days you wake up with a cold, after four hours of sleep, or with legs still wrecked from last week.
Pushing through every time leads to injury and longer illness; skipping every time leads nowhere.
These simple rules, which the [tracker's](/app/) daily check-in uses, help you make the call.

## The rules at a glance

Check them from the top and use the first one that applies.

| How you are | What to do today |
| --- | --- |
| Illness below the neck, or a fever | Rest |
| Under 4.5 hours of sleep | A walk or mobility only |
| Symptoms only above the neck | About 40% fewer sets, keep it short |
| Under 6 hours of sleep, low energy or heavy soreness | About 25% fewer sets and 1 extra rep in reserve |
| None of these | Train as planned |

## The neck check for illness

### Above the neck: light training is usually fine

Symptoms such as a runny or blocked nose, sneezing or a mild sore throat, with no fever, usually allow light training.

- Do about **40% fewer sets** than planned.
- Leave 1 extra rep in reserve on every set and skip any max attempts.
- Keep the session short.
- **Stop if you feel worse as you go.** Feeling a little better after a warm-up is a good sign; feeling worse is a clear signal to go home.
- If you have a cold, be considerate in a shared gym: wipe equipment down, or train at home.

### Below the neck, or a fever: rest

Skip training if you have any of:

- A fever
- Body aches
- Chest congestion or a cough
- Vomiting or diarrhoea
- Extreme fatigue

Exercising with a fever can raise the risk of complications, including inflammation of the heart muscle, and helps spread illness.
In India that includes common seasonal illnesses such as dengue, typhoid and viral fevers, which often need a full recovery period and medical follow-up before training resumes.

### Eating while ill

- **Eat at maintenance.** This is not the time to diet.
- **Keep protein high** to limit muscle loss: dal, curd, eggs, khichdi with extra dal, soups with chicken or paneer.
- **Drink plenty of fluids.** With fever, vomiting or diarrhoea, use oral rehydration solution.

### See a doctor

Get medical help, and do not train, if you have chest pain, trouble breathing, palpitations, or a fever that lasts more than a couple of days.

## Low sleep

One short night does not ruin your progress, but it lowers strength and reaction time and raises injury risk.

### Under 6 hours

You can usually still train.

- Do about **25% fewer sets.**
- Add **1 extra rep in reserve** to every set, so compounds end at 2-4 reps in reserve instead of 1-3.
- Avoid max attempts and highly technical lifts.

The same adjustment applies if your energy is very low or you are heavily sore.

### Under 4.5 hours

Skip the planned session.
Go for a walk, do some mobility work, or rest.
Heavy training on this little sleep has a poor ratio of risk to reward.

### Getting through the day

- **Keep calories and protein normal.** Sleep loss raises hunger, so plan protein-rich snacks such as curd, roasted chana, eggs or a glass of milk before the cravings hit.
- **Caffeine early only,** and none within about 8 hours of bed, so you do not ruin the next night too.
- **A 20-30 minute nap before about 3 pm** helps.
- **Make tonight a priority:** an early bedtime, a dark and cool room, and no screens for an hour before bed.

### When poor sleep keeps going

If poor sleep lasts beyond 1-2 weeks, treat it as a reason to deload: about 40% fewer sets for a week, at similar loads.
Then look honestly at stress, caffeine, alcohol and how late you train.
If it continues, see a doctor.
See [sleep and recovery](/guides/sleep-and-recovery/).

## Heavy soreness

Some soreness after a new exercise or a return from a break is normal and is not a measure of a good workout.
Heavy soreness that limits your range of motion is a reason to ease off: the same 25% fewer sets and 1 extra rep in reserve.
Light movement and walking usually help more than complete rest.
Sharp pain or joint pain is different; stop that exercise and get it checked if it does not settle.

## Coming back after illness

Return **24-48 hours after your symptoms have cleared**, not the moment you feel slightly better.

1. **First sessions:** about 50-70% of your usual sets, with your normal loads or slightly less.
2. **Over the next 1-2 weeks:** build back up to full volume as long as you feel fine.
3. **Expect a small dip in strength.** It usually returns within a week or two.

After a more serious illness, such as dengue, typhoid, COVID-19 or anything that put you in hospital, ask your doctor when and how to return.
Some illnesses need a longer, more gradual build-up.

## Coming back after a break

After a holiday, exams, a wedding season or an injury layoff:

- **Up to about 2 weeks off:** start at about 70-80% of your previous loads and sets, and build back over a week or two.
- **A month or more:** start lighter and treat the first few weeks like a fresh block. Muscle and strength come back much faster than they took to build the first time.
- Expect soreness to return, even on familiar exercises.

The [tracker](/app/) keeps your history, so you can see exactly where you were and climb back with double progression.
See [how to build muscle](/guides/how-to-build-muscle/) for how that works.

## Why this approach works

Missing one session costs you almost nothing.
Training through a fever, or grinding heavy sets on four hours of sleep, can cost you weeks.
Adjusting the day keeps the habit going without digging a hole you then have to climb out of.

This is general information, not medical advice; see a doctor for fever, chest pain, breathing trouble, palpitations, or any illness that does not improve.
