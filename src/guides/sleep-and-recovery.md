Training breaks you down a little; recovery is where you actually adapt.
Two people on the same programme and diet can get very different results because one sleeps seven hours and walks every day while the other sleeps five and lives on stress.
These are the levers worth pulling.

## At a glance

| Factor | Target |
| --- | --- |
| Sleep | 7-9 hours, on a consistent schedule |
| Stress | Some downtime every day |
| Water | About 35 ml per kg, plus sweat losses |
| Steps | About 7,000-10,000 a day |
| Alcohol | Minimal, and not after training |
| Smoking and tobacco | None |
| Health check | Blood work and a check-up every year |

## Sleep

Sleep is when muscle repair, hormone release and memory consolidation do much of their work.
Short sleep raises hunger, lowers strength and reaction time, and raises injury risk.
Most adults need **7-9 hours**.

### Getting more and better sleep

- **Keep a fixed wake-up time,** including weekends. A regular schedule matters as much as total hours.
- **Make the room dark, cool and quiet.** Blackout curtains and an eye mask help in bright cities; set the AC or fan for comfort.
- **No screens for about an hour before bed.** If that is unrealistic, at least dim them and stop scrolling work messages.
- **Caffeine early only,** none within about 8 hours of bed. That includes the evening chai.
- **Avoid a heavy, late dinner.** Leave 2-3 hours between a big meal and sleep.
- **Short naps help.** 20-30 minutes before about 3 pm can rescue a bad night without hurting the next one.

If you snore heavily, wake up gasping, or feel exhausted despite enough hours, see a doctor; sleep apnoea is common and treatable.
On days you slept badly, read [training when sick or tired](/guides/training-when-sick-or-tired/) for how to adjust the session.

## Stress

Work deadlines, exams, family responsibilities and long commutes all draw on the same recovery budget as training.
High, constant stress worsens sleep, slows recovery and often drives overeating.

- Build in daily downtime: a walk without your phone, a hobby, time with friends.
- Try slow breathing for a few minutes: inhale for about 4 seconds, exhale for about 6.
- In a high-stress month, train a little less rather than skipping entirely. Short, easier sessions still count.
- If stress or low mood are persistent, speaking to a counsellor or doctor is a sensible, normal step.

## Hydration

Even mild dehydration lowers strength and focus.

- A good baseline is **35 ml per kg of body weight a day**, so about 2.5 litres for a 70 kg person.
- Add more in heat and on training days. In an Indian summer, sweaty sessions can easily need an extra litre.
- Pale-yellow urine is a simple check.
- For long or very sweaty sessions, add electrolytes: nimbu pani with a pinch of salt, or an ORS sachet.
- Tea, coffee, milk, buttermilk and the water in food all count towards your total.

The [water calculator](/calculators/water/) gives your baseline.

## Alcohol

Alcohol reduces muscle protein synthesis, disrupts sleep quality even when it helps you fall asleep, and adds calories with no nutrition.
A 60 ml large peg of whisky is about 130 kcal before mixers, and a 650 ml bottle of beer is often 250-300 kcal.

- The less, the better; there is no amount that helps training.
- Avoid drinking after a training session, when recovery is at its most active.
- If you do drink, eat a protein-rich meal, alternate with water and stop early enough to sleep well.

## Smoking, vaping and tobacco

Smoking, vaping, gutka and other chewing tobacco hurt lung function, circulation, conditioning and long-term health.
There is no safe level.
Stopping is one of the biggest health improvements anyone can make, and training often makes it easier.
Ask your doctor about support to quit.

## Daily steps

Steps are the cheapest health and recovery tool there is.

- Aim for about **7,000-10,000 steps a day**.
- Walking improves blood flow and recovery, helps control body fat, and does not eat into recovery the way extra intense cardio can.
- A 10-15 minute walk after meals also helps blood sugar control.
- Desk job? Take calls on foot and get up every hour.

See [cardio and heart-rate zones](/guides/cardio-and-heart-rate-zones/) for how walking fits with other cardio.

## Consistency over perfection

Training and eating well 90% of the time beats a perfect plan done for a few weeks.
Recovery works the same way: aim for good habits most nights, not a flawless routine.

## Yearly blood work and check-up

A yearly check catches common issues, like low vitamin D or iron, before they affect your health or training.
Ask your doctor which tests suit you; a typical panel includes:

- **Complete blood count,** including haemoglobin
- **Ferritin,** for iron stores, especially for women
- **Vitamin D** and **vitamin B12**
- **Fasting glucose and HbA1c,** for diabetes risk, which is high in South Asians
- **Lipid profile,** for cholesterol
- **Thyroid function (TSH)**
- **Kidney and liver function**
- **Blood pressure** at the same visit

If you take creatine, tell your doctor.
It can raise blood creatinine, a kidney marker, without any kidney problem, which can be misread.

### Signs you are under-recovered

- Performance falling for two weeks or more
- Persistent soreness or nagging joint pain
- Poor sleep despite being tired
- Resting heart rate higher than usual
- Low motivation or irritability

If these stack up, take an easier week with about 40% fewer sets, and look at sleep, stress and food before adding anything new.
The [tracker](/app/) flags a deload after about six hard weeks or when your main lifts stall.

This is general information, not medical advice; see a doctor about persistent poor sleep, low mood, or any symptom that worries you.
