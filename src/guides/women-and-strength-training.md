Women build strength and muscle in the same way men do, and benefit from it just as much.
Lifting improves body shape, bone density, blood sugar control, mood and confidence at every age.
The same principles apply; this guide covers the questions women ask most often.

## Myths worth dropping

### "Lifting will make me bulky"

Building noticeable muscle takes years of hard, consistent training and deliberate eating, for anyone.
Women have far less testosterone than men and gain muscle more slowly in absolute terms.
What most women actually see from lifting is a firmer, more defined shape and a smaller waist.

### "Women should use light weights and high reps to tone"

"Toning" is building some muscle and losing some fat.
Both happen best with challenging weights taken close to failure, the same as for men.
Pink 1 kg dumbbells for 50 reps do not do much.
Use the rep ranges and effort in [how to build muscle](/guides/how-to-build-muscle/).

### "Cardio is better for fat loss"

Calories drive fat loss; training decides whether you keep muscle while you lose it.
Lifting plus walking usually gives a better result than cardio alone.
See [fat loss](/guides/fat-loss/).

### "Women need a different programme"

Programmes do not need to be different.
Many women like more glute and leg work, and that is a preference, not a requirement.
Women often recover a little faster between sets and can handle similar or slightly higher volume.

## Eating for strength

The numbers in the app work the same way for women:

- Calories use the female Mifflin-St Jeor equation. See [calories and TDEE](/guides/calories-and-tdee/).
- Protein is set per kg of body weight: 2.2 g when cutting, 2.0 g for recomposition, 1.8 g when gaining and 1.6 g when maintaining.
- The calorie target never goes below **1,200 kcal**. Below that, a diet needs medical supervision.

Many Indian women eat far less protein than they need, especially on vegetarian diets.
Adding a protein source to every meal, such as curd, paneer, dal, eggs, chicken, fish, soya or tofu, is usually the single biggest improvement.
See [Indian diet for fitness](/guides/indian-diet-for-fitness/) for portions and sample days.

## The menstrual cycle

### Train as you feel

Some women feel stronger in the first half of the cycle, some feel worse just before or during their period, and many notice little difference.
The research on adjusting training to cycle phases, often called "cycle syncing", is weak and inconsistent.
There is no good evidence that everyone should follow a phase-based plan.

The practical approach:

- **Train normally by default.**
- **On days you feel rough,** do fewer sets or lighter loads, the same way you would after a poor night's sleep. See [training when sick or tired](/guides/training-when-sick-or-tired/).
- **Track your cycle alongside your training** for a few months if you are curious. Patterns that are real for you are worth using.
- **Exercise often helps period pain** for many women, so try a light session before deciding to skip.

### Water weight

Body weight often rises by 1-2 kg before and during a period because of water retention.
It is not fat.
Compare weekly averages from the same point in each cycle, or look at the trend over a full month.

### Missed periods are a warning sign

Missing periods while training hard or dieting is a sign that you are not eating enough for your activity.
It is linked to bone loss and other health problems.
Raise your calories and see a doctor.

## Iron

Iron-deficiency anaemia is widespread among women in India.
Monthly blood loss, vegetarian diets and pregnancy all raise the risk.
Low iron causes tiredness, breathlessness on stairs, poor endurance and slow recovery.

- **Test first.** Ask for haemoglobin and ferritin in your yearly blood work. Ferritin shows iron stores falling before haemoglobin does.
- **Do not take iron without a test.** Excess iron is harmful, and tiredness has many other causes, including thyroid problems and low vitamin B12 or D.
- **Iron-rich foods:** dals, chana, rajma, green leafy vegetables, ragi, jaggery in moderation, eggs, chicken liver, fish and red meat. Iron from animal foods is absorbed more easily.
- **Help absorption:** pair iron-rich meals with vitamin C, such as lemon, amla, guava or tomato. Keep tea and coffee away from meals by about an hour.

See [supplements](/guides/supplements/).

## PCOS

Strength training and regular activity improve insulin sensitivity, which is central to managing PCOS.
Losing even a modest amount of weight, where needed, often helps symptoms.
Work with your doctor on diagnosis and treatment, and use training and diet alongside it, not instead of it.

## Pregnancy and after

**See your doctor or obstetrician before starting or continuing training while pregnant.**

For uncomplicated pregnancies, the WHO recommends regular physical activity, including at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week and some muscle strengthening.
In general:

- Women who already lift can often continue with guidance, reducing load and intensity as pregnancy progresses.
- Avoid exercises with a risk of falls or blows to the abdomen, lying flat on your back for long periods later in pregnancy, and training to exhaustion.
- Stay well hydrated and avoid overheating, especially in Indian summers.
- Stop and seek medical advice for bleeding, dizziness, chest pain, calf pain or swelling, fluid leakage or regular painful contractions.

After birth, get clearance from your doctor before returning.
Pelvic floor and core rehabilitation, ideally with a women's health physiotherapist, is a good first step, especially after a caesarean.

## Menopause and bone health

Around menopause, falling oestrogen speeds up loss of bone and muscle.
Osteoporosis and fractures become much more common in later life.
Strength training is one of the most effective tools against both.

- **Lift progressively heavier weights.** Bones respond to load, so challenging weights matter more than light ones.
- **Add some impact** if your joints and doctor allow: brisk walking, stair climbing, skipping or small jumps.
- **Keep protein high,** at least 1.6 g per kg, to protect muscle.
- **Get enough calcium and vitamin D.** Aim for about 1,000 mg of calcium a day from milk, curd, paneer, ragi, sesame and green leafy vegetables, and test your vitamin D.
- **Train balance** with single-leg exercises to reduce the risk of falls.
- **Ask your doctor about a bone density scan** if you have risk factors such as early menopause, low body weight or a family history of osteoporosis.

It is never too late to start.
Women who begin lifting in their 50s, 60s and beyond still gain strength and improve their bone health.

## Getting started

If you are new, follow the [beginner's guide](/guides/beginners-guide/) and log your sessions in the [tracker](/app/).
Three full-body sessions a week, with progressive weights and enough protein, is all it takes to begin.

This is general information, not medical advice; see a doctor before training if you are pregnant, postpartum, have PCOS, heavy or missed periods, or any medical condition.
