Muscle growth is not mysterious, and it does not need a secret programme.
It comes from a handful of variables done well for months and years: hard sets, enough of them, spread sensibly across the week, with the load creeping up over time.
This guide covers those variables and how to put them together.

## Mechanical tension: the main driver

A muscle grows when it is made to work against a challenging load, close to the point where it cannot do another rep.
That combination of heavy-enough weight and high effort is called mechanical tension.

### Rep ranges

Growth happens across a wide range, roughly 5-30 reps a set, as long as the set is genuinely hard.
In practice most of your training can sit in the middle.

| Type of work | Typical reps | Rough load |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heavy compounds (squat, bench, deadlift) | 3-6 | 85% of your one-rep max and up |
| Most training | 6-15 | About 60-85% of your one-rep max |
| Isolation and machines | 15-30 | Lighter, taken very close to failure |

If you want to know your one-rep max without testing it, the [one-rep max calculator](/calculators/one-rep-max/) estimates it from a normal working set.

### How hard is hard enough

Effort is measured in reps in reserve (RIR): how many more clean reps you could have done when you stopped.

- **Compound lifts** such as squats, presses, rows and Romanian deadlifts: stop at 1-3 RIR. Grinding these to failure costs a lot of recovery and invites sloppy form.
- **Isolation and machine work** such as curls, lateral raises and leg extensions: go to 0-1 RIR. Failure here is safe and cheap to recover from.

A set that ends with five easy reps left in the tank does very little.

### Technique that adds tension

- Use a full range of motion.
- Lower the weight under control, about 2-3 seconds, with no bouncing.
- Favour exercises that load the muscle in a stretched position, such as incline dumbbell curls, Romanian deadlifts and overhead triceps extensions.
- Rest 2-3 minutes between compound sets and 60-90 seconds between isolation sets, so the next set is still hard for the right reasons.

## Volume: how many hard sets

Volume is counted as hard sets per muscle group per week.
Only sets taken within about 3 reps of failure count.

| Training age | Hard sets per muscle per week |
| --- | --- |
| Beginner (under a year) | 6-10 |
| Intermediate | 10-16 |
| Advanced | 12-20 or more |

Start at the low end of your band.
Add 1-2 sets per muscle every week or two, but only while your lifts keep improving.
Once recovery falls behind, more sets make things worse, not better.

## Frequency: how often to train each muscle

Training each muscle about twice a week works well for most people.
Total weekly sets matter more than the exact split, but spreading them out has a real benefit: the last few sets of a 20-set session for one muscle are low quality, while the same sets split across two days are not.
Advanced lifters sometimes go to three times a week to fit in more volume.

## Progressive overload with double progression

Your muscles only keep adapting if the challenge keeps rising.
The simplest reliable way to do that is double progression, which is what the [tracker](/app/) uses.

1. Pick a rep range for the exercise, for example 8-12.
2. Use the same load each session and try to add reps.
3. When every set reaches the top of the range, add the smallest load step available next session.
4. Drop back to the bottom of the range with the new load and build up again.

For bodyweight moves with no load to add, keep adding reps, then switch to a harder variation or slow the lowering to about 4 seconds.

Log every working set.
Without a record you are guessing, and guessing tends to drift towards the same weights for months.

## Splits by days per week

Pick the split that fits the days you can reliably show up, not the days you wish you had.

| Days a week | Split | Example layout |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | Full body | Mon A, Wed B, Fri C (rotate) |
| 4 | Upper / lower | Mon upper, Tue lower, Thu upper, Fri lower |
| 5 | Upper / lower plus push / pull / legs | Upper, lower, push, pull, legs |
| 6 | Push / pull / legs twice | Each muscle twice a week; needs good recovery |

Three well-run full-body days will build more muscle than six half-hearted ones.
If you are new to lifting, start with three days and read the [beginner's guide](/guides/beginners-guide/).

## Managing fatigue

### Autoregulate

If bar speed drops and a normal working weight feels like a max, cut the volume for that day rather than pushing through.
The tracker's daily check-in adjusts your session for poor sleep, soreness or illness; see [training when sick or tired](/guides/training-when-sick-or-tired/).

### Deload

Fatigue builds quietly over weeks.
Take an easier week after about 6 weeks of hard training, or sooner if your main lifts stall.
During a deload, do about 40% fewer sets and keep the loads roughly the same.
You will usually come back stronger.

### Warning signs of overreaching

- Performance falling for two weeks or more
- Soreness that does not clear
- Poor sleep or a raised resting heart rate
- Low motivation and nagging joint pain

### Joint health

Warm up with lighter ramp-up sets before your first heavy exercise.
Vary exercises and grips from block to block.
Never train through sharp pain; dull muscular effort is normal, sharp or joint pain is not.

## Recovery and food

Training is the stimulus, but growth happens while you recover.
That means 7-9 hours of sleep, enough calories and protein, and not stacking hard leg days back to back.
For the eating side, read [macros explained](/guides/macros-explained/) and [lean bulk](/guides/lean-bulk/), and see [sleep and recovery](/guides/sleep-and-recovery/) for the rest.

## Common mistakes

- Chasing "muscle confusion" with new exercises every week instead of adding load or reps
- Changing the programme every two weeks before it has a chance to work
- Skipping legs, sleep and deloads
- Stopping sets far from failure
- Eating too little protein or too few calories while trying to grow

This is general information, not medical advice; see a doctor before starting if you have a medical condition or an injury.
